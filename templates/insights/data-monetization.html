How Most Terms of Service Let Companies Make Money From Your Data

Most free apps and websites don’t charge you a monthly fee. That doesn’t mean you’re not paying. In many cases, you are the product — and the details are buried in the Terms of Service and Privacy Policy.

Here’s how it typically works, in plain English.

1. They collect far more than you think

Most policies allow the company to gather:

  • Account details (name, email, phone)
  • Usage data (what you click, how long you stay, what you search)
  • Device information (phone type, browser, IP address)
  • Location data
  • Information from cookies and tracking tools
  • Data from other companies (partners, advertisers, data brokers)

One app rarely sees only what you type into it. It often builds a broader profile over time.

2. “Sharing with partners” often means selling access to your attention

You’ll frequently see language like:

  • “We may share information with third-party partners”
  • “We work with advertising networks”
  • “We may disclose data for business purposes”

In practice, this often means advertisers pay to show you targeted ads, data brokers buy or license audience segments, and analytics tools get access to behavioral data. The company earns revenue from that ecosystem — even if you never buy anything from them directly.

You’re not always sold as a named individual. More often, you’re packaged into groups: “people interested in X,” “users in this zip code,” “likely to buy Y.” That still has real commercial value.

3. Your content and activity can become a product too

Some terms go further. They may give the company broad rights to:

  • Use content you upload
  • Analyze your messages or files to improve their systems
  • Train AI models on user data
  • Create derived insights they can monetize

Even when they say they don’t “sell your personal information,” they may still profit from aggregated, anonymized, or “de-identified” data — or from advertising systems built on top of it.

4. “Improving our services” is a very wide umbrella

Almost every privacy policy includes a purpose like “to improve our products and services.” That can legitimately mean bug fixes and better features. It can also cover building richer user profiles, testing what keeps you engaged longer, developing new data products, and feeding machine-learning systems that power ads or recommendations. The phrase is flexible by design.

5. Free is rarely free

If a service is free and has no clear alternative revenue model (hardware, premium seats, etc.), data and attention are usually part of the business model. That doesn’t make every company evil — but it does mean the incentives are not the same as a product you pay for outright.

Questions worth asking before you click “I Agree”

  • Can they share or sell data to advertisers and partners?
  • Do they claim broad rights to use content you create?
  • Is “improving services” defined clearly, or left vague?
  • Can you opt out of targeted advertising or data sharing?
  • What happens to your data if the company is sold?

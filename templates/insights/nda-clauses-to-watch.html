NDAs in Plain English: 5 Clauses to Watch Before You Sign

NDAs are common for freelancers, consultants, and small businesses. They’re not automatically unfair — but a few clauses can change what you’re really agreeing to.

A non-disclosure agreement (NDA) is supposed to protect confidential information. In practice, many NDAs also touch ownership of work, how long silence lasts, and what you can still use from your own skill and experience.

You don’t need to reject every NDA. You do need to notice the parts that go beyond “don’t share our secrets.”

Here are five clauses worth reading carefully before you sign.

1. What counts as “confidential”

Some NDAs define confidential information narrowly: specific documents, code, customer lists, or trade secrets marked as confidential.

Others define it so broadly that almost anything you see or hear could count — including information that is public, already known to you, or independently developed.

Why it matters: An over-broad definition makes it easier to claim you breached the NDA later, even when you acted reasonably.

What to watch for: Definitions that include “all information related to the business” with weak exceptions for public or pre-existing knowledge.

2. How long the obligation lasts

Confidentiality doesn’t always end when the project ends. Many NDAs continue for a set number of years — or, for true trade secrets, as long as the information stays secret.

That’s normal for real secrets. It’s harder to justify when ordinary business information is locked down for a very long time with no clear limit.

Why it matters: A long or indefinite term can follow you into the next client relationship.

What to watch for: “Perpetual” obligations applied to ordinary information, not only trade secrets.

3. Who owns the work (and improvements)

NDAs are about secrecy. Some also slip in ownership language: anything you create, improve, or suggest in connection with the engagement belongs to the other party.

That can be appropriate for work product you were hired to deliver. It becomes risky when it reaches your pre-existing tools, templates, methods, or general know-how.

Why it matters: You can accidentally give away rights to things you brought with you or reuse in your business.

What to watch for: Assignment or “work made for hire” language inside an NDA that goes beyond the confidential information itself.

4. Residuals and “unaided memory” — what you can still use

A residuals clause (sometimes called an unaided memory clause) deals with what stays in your head after the project: general skills, know-how, and ideas you remember without taking files, notes, or copies.

Some NDAs allow residual use. That means you can keep using general knowledge in future work. Others try to restrict even what you remember, which is hard to apply fairly and can conflict with how freelancers and consultants actually work.

Wording that should make you pause looks like this:

“The Receiving Party shall not use any Confidential Information retained in unaided memory, including general skills, know-how, or ideas, in any later engagement.”

A more workable version usually allows use of residual knowledge that is not copied from documents and is part of your ordinary professional skill.

Why it matters: Freelancers and consultants build careers on cumulative skill. A harsh residuals / unaided-memory stance can create fear around normal future work.

What to watch for: Language that treats any remembered idea as off-limits, without a practical residual-use allowance.

5. One-sided duties and harsh remedies

Many NDAs are mutual. Some are one-way: only you have duties; the company has few or none.

Also watch remedies: automatic injunctions, broad liability, or terms that make any dispute expensive for you to contest. Strong remedies can be legitimate for real secrets — they should still match the risk.

Why it matters: A one-sided NDA with aggressive remedies shifts leverage heavily before any real dispute exists.

What to watch for: Only one party bound; extreme penalty-style language; no practical exceptions.

A simple before-you-sign checklist

  • Is “confidential” defined clearly, with fair exceptions?
  • How long do obligations last — and for what kind of information?
  • Does the NDA also claim ownership of your work or tools?
  • Can you still use general skill and residual knowledge?
  • Are duties mutual, and are remedies proportionate?

NDAs are part of doing business. Reading these five areas doesn’t replace a lawyer on a high-stakes deal — it does help you notice when a “standard NDA” is doing more than protecting secrets.

NDA clause FAQs

What is a residuals clause in an NDA?

It says whether you may keep using general knowledge that stays in your unaided memory after the job ends. It should not be a back-door ban on using your own professional skill.

What does “unaided memory” mean?

Information you remember without notes, files, screenshots, or other copies. Many NDAs allow that kind of residual knowledge; some try to forbid it.

Can an NDA take ownership of my work?

Some NDAs slip in assignment or “work made for hire” language. That can be appropriate for work you were hired to deliver. It is risky when it reaches tools, templates, or methods you already had.

Are one-sided duties and harsh remedies normal?

One-way NDAs exist. Strong remedies can be legitimate for real trade secrets. They should still match the risk — especially for a freelancer reviewing a “standard” form.

The 6-Month Silence Clause: What Exclusivity Really Costs Creators

A brand deal looks simple on the surface: post about the product, get paid. Then you hit the exclusivity section.

Some contracts quietly tell you that you cannot post about competing products — or sometimes any other brand — for months after the campaign ends. Six months is a number that shows up often enough that creators notice it.

What the clause is actually doing

Exclusivity is the brand buying temporary protection. They do not want your audience seeing a competitor’s product while their campaign is still active, or right after it ends.

There are a few common versions:

  • Named-competitor exclusivity — You cannot post for a short list of specific brands.
  • Category exclusivity — You cannot post for any brand in a defined category (skincare, energy drinks, fintech apps, etc.).
  • Full exclusivity — You cannot take other paid brand work at all during the window.

The broader the definition and the longer the window, the more it can limit what you earn.

Why 6 months shows up

Thirty to ninety days is common for a single post or short campaign. Six months is more typical of ambassador or retainer-style deals, but it also appears in one-off contracts that do not pay like one.

The problem is usually not the idea of exclusivity. It is when the restriction is long, the category is vague (“any beauty brand”), and the fee does not go up to match the lost work.

What to check before you sign

  • How long does the restriction last, and does it start on signing or on the post date?
  • Is it limited to paid/sponsored posts, or does it also cover organic mentions?
  • Is the category named tightly, or is it broad enough to block most of your potential deals?
  • Is there a separate fee for the exclusivity window, or is it baked into a flat rate that does not account for it?
  • What happens if the brand cancels or delays the campaign — does the restriction still apply?

A practical way to read it

Treat the exclusivity period like inventory you are selling. If the brand wants six months of category protection, the contract should make that trade visible — either a higher fee or a narrower window.

Most of these terms are negotiable. The clauses that cause the most problems are the ones people skip because the rest of the deal looks fine.
